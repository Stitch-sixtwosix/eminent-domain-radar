The agency's written offer: how it is built and how to read it

The written offer is often the first document an owner takes seriously. It carries a figure, it looks official and it usually arrives with a request to respond. Owners tend to read it either as a final answer or as a casual opening bid. It is generally neither. The figure comes out of a defined process, and knowing that process tells counsel what to ask for and where to look.

Where the figure comes from

Federal law sets acquisition policies for projects that use federal money, and most states have comparable requirements of their own. The details differ, but the usual sequence is similar. The agency has the property appraised before negotiations begin, and the owner is generally given the opportunity to accompany the appraiser on the inspection. The appraisal is reviewed inside the agency or by a review appraiser. The agency then sets an amount it believes to be just compensation, and the offer is made in writing at that amount.

Two points follow. The offer is normally tied to an approved appraisal, so the negotiator who delivers it may have limited authority to move without new information. And for acquisitions the agency regards as low in value and uncomplicated, many programmes allow a simplified valuation in place of a full appraisal. It is worth asking which was used.

What should come with it

An offer usually arrives with supporting documents, and the first task is to see whether the set is complete. Look for:

  • A summary of the basis for the offer. A statement identifying the property, the interest being acquired and how the amount was reached.
  • A breakdown. In a partial acquisition, the amount for the part acquired and any amount for damage to the remainder are commonly stated separately.
  • A description of the acquisition. Plan sheets and a legal description showing the area and the type of interest, whether fee, permanent easement or temporary easement.
  • A list of what is included. Buildings, fixtures and site improvements the agency treats as part of the real estate.

Whether the owner is entitled to the full appraisal report, or only to a summary, varies by state and sometimes by agency. If the report was not provided, ask for it and note the response.

What the offer usually leaves out

The offer addresses the real estate. Relocation assistance is generally handled separately, by a different person and under different rules. Business losses, where a state recognises them at all, are often outside the offer as well. An owner comparing the figure with everything the project will cost them is usually not comparing like with like, and that should be explained early.

Checking the offer against the plans

Before responding on value, confirm that the offer describes the right acquisition. Compare the area and interests in the offer with the current plan sheets. Designs are revised, and an appraisal may have been prepared on an earlier version. Check that every improvement inside the acquisition area is accounted for, and whether the valuation considered the remainder or treated the matter as the purchase of a strip.

Note the date of the appraisal as well. A long interval between the valuation and the offer may matter in a changing market, and how that is dealt with depends on the jurisdiction.

Responding

Many states require the agency to make a genuine attempt to negotiate before it files a condemnation action. Agencies generally can revise an offer when given information the appraisal did not reflect, and some have a formal process for settling above the appraised amount. A response that identifies a specific omission or error, with documents, gives the agency something it can act on. A bare counter-figure usually does not.

Record the date the offer was received and any period stated for a response. In some states the offer also has consequences later in the case, for example in how interest, costs or fees are treated, so keep the offer and everything that came with it on file.

This article is general information, not legal advice. Eminent domain law differs by state.

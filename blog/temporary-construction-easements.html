Temporary construction easements: what to read before valuing one

Many road and utility projects need more room to build than they need to keep. The agency acquires a strip permanently and, beside it, a temporary construction easement: the right to use a further area while the work is done. The land comes back to the owner afterwards, the figure attached to it in the offer is usually the smallest on the page, and it tends to receive the least attention. Sometimes that is reasonable. Sometimes the temporary easement is what disrupts the property most.

What the agency acquires

A temporary construction easement gives the agency and its contractor the use of a defined area for a limited time. Typical purposes are grading slopes to meet the new road, rebuilding driveways, working room beside a structure or a pipe, and storing equipment and materials. The owner keeps title and gets possession back when the easement ends. The extent of the right depends on the easement document, not on the label.

How one is generally valued

In general terms a temporary easement is valued by what the owner loses during the period it is in force. Appraisers commonly start from the rental value of the area for the term. To that may be added the value of improvements in the area that will be removed or damaged, and any loss the easement causes to the rest of the property while it lasts.

States differ on the details, including which temporary effects on the remainder are compensable and how they are measured. Inconvenience during construction, taken alone, is treated very differently from one state to another. Confirm the approach the jurisdiction uses before assessing the agency's figure.

The terms that matter

The easement document and the plan sheets should answer several questions. Where they do not, ask the agency in writing.

  • Start. Whether the term runs from a fixed date, from the date of acquisition, from the start of construction or from notice to the owner.
  • Length. How long the easement lasts, and whether the agency can extend it or let it run until the project is complete.
  • Permitted use. Whether the area may be used only for the work described or also for storage, staging and contractor access.
  • Exclusivity. Whether the owner may continue to use the area, and cross it, while the easement is in force.
  • Restoration. What condition the area is to be left in, and who replaces paving, fencing, landscaping, irrigation and signs.

An easement with a short stated term but an uncertain start can affect the property for much longer than the term suggests. An owner who cannot say when the work will begin may find it harder to lease, sell or build on the affected part in the meantime. Whether that is compensable depends on state law, but it should be identified.

What sits in the area

A plan sheet shows the easement as a shaded strip. Walk it. Parking spaces, a drive aisle, a loading area, a sign, a well, a septic field or mature trees inside the strip change the analysis. Losing a row of parking for a construction season may matter more to a business than the permanent loss of a narrow strip of frontage.

Check access as well. If the easement crosses the only entrance, ask how the site will be reached while the work is done and whether that is recorded anywhere.

Tenants and the lease

Where the property is leased, the tenant is often the one who bears a temporary easement. Read the lease for what it says about a partial or temporary taking, whether rent abates and who is entitled to the payment. A landlord who settles the easement without regard to the lease may face a claim from the tenant later.

What to ask at intake

Ask the owner how the easement area is used day to day and at what times of year it matters most. Collect the easement document, the plan sheets and any construction schedule the agency has provided. Photograph the area before work begins.

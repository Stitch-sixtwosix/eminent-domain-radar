The first meeting with an owner: what to establish at intake

An owner who calls about a public project usually wants to know one thing: what the property is worth to the agency. That question cannot be answered at a first meeting. What can be done is to establish where the matter stands, who has an interest in the property and what the owner should avoid doing in the meantime. A consistent intake covers those points every time.

Where the project stands

Start with what the owner has received and from whom. A newsletter about a corridor study, a request to enter the land for survey, a written offer and a filed petition are very different stages, and owners do not always distinguish between them.

Ask for every document, including envelopes and anything left at the door. Then check the agency's own records for the project's current status. The owner's account and the public record should agree, and where they do not, the difference is worth understanding before any advice is given.

Who holds an interest

The person who calls is not always the only person with a claim, or even the record owner. Establish early:

  • Title. How the property is held, and whether co-owners, a trust, an estate or a business entity is involved.
  • Lenders. Whether there is a mortgage or other lien, since a lender will usually have a say in how proceeds are applied.
  • Tenants. Who occupies the property, under what lease, and what the lease says about condemnation.
  • Others. Easement holders, option holders and anyone farming or using the land by informal arrangement.

This also determines who the client is. Co-owners, landlords and tenants can have interests that diverge when an award is divided, so run the conflict check with every name before taking the matter.

What has already been said and signed

Ask what contact the owner has had with the agency or its right-of-way agent. Owners sometimes sign a right-of-entry form, answer an appraiser's questions or state a figure in conversation without thinking of it as part of a negotiation. None of that is necessarily a problem, but counsel needs to know about it at the outset.

The property as it is used

The plan sheet shows an area. The owner knows how the property works. Ask how vehicles enter and leave, what sits in or near the area to be acquired, what improvements were made recently and what the owner intended to do with the land. Any plans for development, expansion or sale that were under way before the project became known are relevant to value and should be documented now.

A site visit with photographs is worth arranging early, while the property is still in its before condition.

Dates

Condemnation procedure runs on time limits, and they differ by state and by stage. An offer may be open for a stated period. Once a case is filed, the time to respond or to challenge the taking can be short. Record the date on every document the owner brings and identify the applicable limits before the meeting ends.

What to tell the owner

A first meeting should leave the owner with a small number of clear instructions:

  • Keep every document and note every call or visit from the agency.
  • Do not sign anything or agree a figure without advice.
  • Do not move out, end a lease or make major changes to the property because of the project without discussing it first.
  • Keep the property maintained and insured as usual.

It should also leave the owner with an accurate picture. Explain how the process generally unfolds in the jurisdiction, that timing is largely in the agency's hands, and that no one can say at this stage what the result will be. Put the fee arrangement and the scope of the engagement in writing, including anything the firm is not handling.

This article is general information, not legal advice. Eminent domain law differs by state.

What a planning list tells you years before a condemnation is filed

Most property owners learn about a taking when a right-of-way agent calls or a letter arrives. By then the route is fixed, the appraisal is under way and the agency has had years to prepare. The owner has had none.

The project itself was not a secret during those years. It was sitting on a public list.

Where projects first appear

Transportation departments, transit authorities, utilities and cities are required to plan capital projects in public. A highway widening usually shows up first in a long-range program, then moves into a shorter funded program as money is assigned, then into environmental review, design and right-of-way acquisition. Each step produces public records.

  • Long-range programs name the project, its limits and a rough cost. Nothing is designed yet.
  • Funded programs add a schedule and a budget by phase. A line for right-of-way funding is a strong sign that land will be bought.
  • Environmental documents describe the alternatives and often include maps precise enough to see which parcels each alternative touches.
  • Local legislation authorising acquisition or condemnation frequently lists the parcels by number.

What the early records do not tell you

A planning list is not a notice of taking. Projects are delayed, redesigned and cancelled. A route drawn on a planning map can shift before design is final, and a parcel that abuts the line today may be untouched in the final plan. Anyone using these records has to say so plainly, especially when writing to an owner.

Why the timing matters to counsel

An owner who understands the process before the first offer is in a different position from one who learns it afterwards. They know an appraisal is coming, they know the first number is an opening position, and they know they are entitled to their own advice. None of that requires a dispute. It requires knowing early.

The practical problem has always been volume. A single state program can hold hundreds of projects, and tracing each one to the parcels along it is slow work. That is the work EminentTracker automates: it reads the lists each day, maps the routes onto county parcel records and shows who owns the land beside them.

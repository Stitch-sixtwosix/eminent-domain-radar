Tenants in a taking: what the lease decides before anyone negotiates

When a public project reaches a leased property, two parties have something to lose and only one of them is usually on the agency's mailing list. The landlord holds title and receives the offer. The tenant may hold years of remaining term, improvements paid for out of its own pocket and a location its customers know. How those interests are treated depends on state law and, to a large degree, on a paragraph in the lease that neither side has read since signing.

A leasehold is an interest in property

As a general matter, a tenant under a valid lease holds a property interest, and the taking of that interest can be compensable. The measure and the procedure vary by state. In many jurisdictions the property is valued as a whole, as though one owner held it, and the award is then divided among those with an interest. In others the interests may be valued separately. Which approach applies affects who needs to be at the table and when, so it is one of the first things to confirm.

The condemnation clause

Most commercial leases, and some residential ones, say what happens if the property is taken. They commonly address:

  • Termination. Whether the lease ends automatically on a taking, ends at one party's option, or continues.
  • The award. Whether the tenant keeps any claim to the compensation or assigns all of it to the landlord.
  • Reserved claims. Whether the tenant may still pursue items such as moving costs, its own fixtures and equipment, or relocation assistance, so long as doing so does not reduce the landlord's recovery.
  • Partial takings. Whether rent is reduced, who restores the premises, and at what point a partial loss allows either side to end the lease.

A clause that terminates the lease and gives the whole award to the landlord can leave a tenant with little claim against the compensation for the real estate, however long the remaining term. Courts generally enforce such clauses as written, though how strictly they are read differs by state. Where the lease is silent, the default rules of the jurisdiction apply.

Where a tenant's value usually lies

A leasehold tends to have compensable value when the rent the tenant pays is below what the space would now command, because the remaining term is then worth something in the market. Tenant-installed improvements and trade fixtures raise a separate question: whether they are treated as part of the real estate, and if so, who is paid for them. The lease, the way the items are attached and state law all bear on the answer. Loss of business or goodwill is recoverable in some states and not in others.

Relocation assistance, where it applies, runs on its own track and is generally not something a lease assigns to the landlord. It should be considered for every displaced tenant regardless of what the clause says about the award.

Partial takings and the lease that continues

When only part of the property is acquired, the lease often survives. The tenant may remain bound to a site with less parking, altered access or a shorter frontage. Whether rent abates, and by how much, is again a matter of the clause and state law. A tenant in this position should understand its options before the landlord settles with the agency, since the settlement may fix the facts the tenant has to live with.

What this means for counsel

For landlord or tenant, the lease belongs in the file at the first meeting, along with every amendment, renewal and estoppel. Because the two sides may end up dividing a single award, their interests can diverge, and joint representation needs careful thought under the conflict rules of the state.

It also bears on who to look for. The owner of record is one affected party. The business operating at the address may be another, with its own questions and no notice yet.

The larger parcel: deciding what counts as the whole property

Before anyone can measure what a partial taking does to the land left behind, someone has to decide what the land left behind is. An owner may hold several lots, some bought at different times, some in a different name, some across a road. Whether they are valued as one property or as separate ones is the larger parcel question. In an appraisal of a partial taking it is usually answered early, often in a paragraph, and it can matter more than any adjustment made later.

Why it matters

Damage to the remainder is generally measured against the property of which the acquired area formed a part. If the strip is treated as coming from a single lot, only that lot is examined for damage. If the lot is treated as part of a wider holding used together, the effect on the whole holding is in question. The same definition sets the frame for highest and best use and for the choice of comparable sales, since an assembled site and a single lot do not sell in the same market.

A wider definition does not always favour the owner. Where a state allows benefits from the project to be set against damages, a wider parcel can bring more benefited land into the calculation. The aim is the definition that is accurate on the facts.

The three factors

Courts and appraisers generally look at three things, often called the three unities:

  • Ownership. Whether the tracts are held by the same owner or under the same control.
  • Use. Whether the tracts are used together as a single economic unit, or are reasonably likely to be.
  • Physical connection. Whether the tracts adjoin one another.

States weigh them differently. In many, unity of use carries the most weight. Some treat contiguity as close to a requirement, while others accept tracts separated by a road or by other land where they are operated as one. Some require identical ownership, and others look at who controls and benefits from the land. Whether the question is decided by the judge or by the jury also varies. Confirm each of these points for the jurisdiction before relying on any definition.

Where the disputes arise

Most disagreements fall into a few recurring patterns:

  • Related owners. One lot is held personally and the adjoining lot by a company or trust the same person controls. Whether that counts as one owner for this purpose depends on state law.
  • Separated tracts. A farm with fields on both sides of a road, or a business with its parking on a lot across the street.
  • Platted lots. Land subdivided on paper but used as one site, or the reverse, where one tax parcel holds two unrelated uses.
  • Planned use. Tracts not yet used together but assembled for a single project. The evidence needed is similar to that for a highest and best use that differs from the present one.
  • A smaller parcel. Sometimes the accurate definition is narrower than the record parcel, for example where part of a tract is ready for development and the rest is not.

Reading the agency's appraisal

Find the section that identifies the larger parcel and read it against a map of everything the owner holds nearby. An appraisal may start from the tax parcel or the parcel number on the plan sheet, which follows the records and not necessarily the way the land is used. If adjoining land under the same or related ownership is not mentioned, ask whether it was considered. Check too that the highest and best use conclusion and the comparable sales match the parcel as defined.

What to ask at intake

Ask the owner what other land they, their family or their companies own or lease in the area, and how each tract is used with the others. Useful records include deeds, entity documents, leases between related owners, site plans, and anything showing shared access, utilities, parking or operations. Give the appraiser the legal standard the state applies and the facts that bear on it, so that the report defines the property on the right premise from the start.

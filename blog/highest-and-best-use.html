Highest and best use: the question that frames every valuation

Two appraisers can inspect the same property, use sound methods and arrive at very different figures. Often the reason is not the comparable sales or the arithmetic. It is that they answered an earlier question differently: what use of this property should be valued? That question is highest and best use, and in many condemnation matters it accounts for more of the gap than anything else.

The general idea

Just compensation is generally measured by market value, and a buyer in the market pays for what a property can reasonably become, not only for what it is today. A house on a commercial corridor, a farm at the edge of a growing town and a vacant lot beside an interchange may each be worth more for a use other than the present one. The law in most states accordingly allows the property to be valued at its highest and best use, provided that use is shown to be reasonably probable and not speculative.

The four tests

Appraisers usually work through four questions, and a proposed use has to pass all of them:

  • Legally permissible. Zoning, land use plans, deed restrictions, environmental rules and access regulations must allow the use, or a change must be reasonably probable.
  • Physically possible. The size, shape, topography, soils, drainage and utilities of the site must be able to support it.
  • Financially feasible. There must be enough demand in the market for the use to produce a return that justifies its cost.
  • Maximally productive. Of the uses that pass the first three tests, it must be the one that yields the highest value.

The analysis is commonly done twice, once for the land as though vacant and once for the property as improved. Where the two answers differ, the existing building may be contributing little to value, and that affects which sales are comparable.

Where the disputes arise

Most disagreements fall into a few recurring areas:

  • Rezoning. When the proposed use needs a zoning change, the question is how likely that change was on the date of valuation. States differ on the showing required and on how the uncertainty is reflected in the figure. Generally the property is valued as a buyer would value it with that prospect, not as though the change had already been granted.
  • Timing. A use that the market will not support for many years is not the same as one that is ready now. An interim use, with a later change, is sometimes the more accurate description.
  • Evidence. A stated intention to develop carries little weight alone. Site plans, applications, engineering work, inquiries from buyers and the pattern of development on neighbouring land carry more.
  • The project itself. Many jurisdictions exclude changes in value caused by the public project for which the property is being taken, whether up or down. How that rule is applied, and from what date, varies and should be checked.

Reading the agency's appraisal

An agency report will state a highest and best use conclusion, sometimes in a few lines. Read that section before the sales. If it concludes that the present use is the highest and best use, look for the reasoning. Check whether surrounding properties have changed use, whether the local plan designates the area for something else, and whether the comparable sales were chosen to match the stated use. In a partial taking the question is asked again for the remainder. A site that supported a given use before the acquisition may no longer meet the size, setback or access requirements for it afterwards, and that change is often where the damage lies.

What to collect early

Ask the owner what they planned for the property and what exists on paper to show it. Obtain the zoning and the land use plan designation, any applications filed or approvals received, and details of recent changes of use nearby. If a higher use is going to be asserted, a land planner or engineer may be needed to support it, and the appraiser should have that work before forming an opinion.

This article is general information, not legal advice. Eminent domain law differs by state.

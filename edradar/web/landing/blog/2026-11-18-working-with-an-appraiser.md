In most condemnation matters the disagreement is about value, and value is proved through appraisers. The owner's lawyer does not form the opinion, but the lawyer decides when an appraiser is brought in, what the appraiser is asked and what information reaches them. Those decisions shape the report long before anyone testifies.

## When to bring one in

There is a case for early involvement. The property can be inspected and photographed in its before condition, which is hard to reconstruct once construction starts. An appraiser who sees the site early can also say whether the matter justifies a full report, or whether the agency's figure is within a reasonable range.

Many lawyers begin with a limited consultation and commission a written report only if the numbers support it. How an appraiser is retained can also affect whether their work and communications are discoverable, and the rules on consulting and testifying experts vary by state, so settle the role before much is put in writing.

## Choosing the right appraiser

Condemnation work is a specialty. Points worth checking include:

- **Property type.** Experience with the kind of property involved, whether a farm, a convenience store or an industrial site.
- **Local market.** Familiarity with sales and land use in the area.
- **Condemnation experience.** Knowledge of the valuation method the jurisdiction uses for partial takings, and experience testifying.
- **Licensing.** The credential the state requires for the assignment.
- **Conflicts.** Whether the appraiser also works for the acquiring agency, and whether that matters to the client.

## The instructions matter

An appraisal answers the question it is asked. The lawyer supplies the legal framework: the date of valuation, the property interest being acquired, what counts as the whole property, and which elements of damage the jurisdiction recognises. These are legal questions with answers that differ by state, and an appraiser left to guess may produce a careful report on the wrong premise.

The instruction stops there. The opinion of value has to be the appraiser's own. Professional standards require independence, and the appraiser's fee cannot depend on the figure reached. A report that reads as advocacy is less useful in negotiation and at trial than one that is plainly independent.

## What to give them

The appraiser needs the same facts the agency's appraiser had, and usually more. Useful material includes:

- The agency's plans, the description of the acquisition and any offer documents
- Surveys, site plans and title information
- Leases, rent rolls and income and expense records for income-producing property
- Zoning status, permits, approvals and any development plans the owner had under way
- The owner's account of how the property is used day to day

Owners often know things no record shows, such as how delivery trucks turn in or which part of a field floods. Arrange for the appraiser to hear it directly.

## Reading the agency's appraisal

The agency's report deserves a close reading before anyone responds to an offer. Common points of difference are:

- **Highest and best use.** Whether the report values the property for its most valuable reasonably probable use or only its current one.
- **Comparable sales.** How similar the sales are, how recent, and how they were adjusted.
- **The larger parcel.** Whether the report treats the right land as the whole property.
- **The remainder.** Whether damage to the remaining property was analysed or passed over.
- **The plans relied on.** Whether the report reflects the current design.

A difference on any one of these can account for most of the gap between two opinions. Identifying which one is in play tells the lawyer what the negotiation is about.

## Other experts

An appraiser often relies on others. An engineer may address access, grading or drainage, a land planner may speak to what could be built on the remainder, and a contractor may estimate the cost to cure. Involve them early enough for the appraiser to use their conclusions.

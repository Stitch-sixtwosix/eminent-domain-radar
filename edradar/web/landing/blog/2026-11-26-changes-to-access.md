Owners along a road project often worry less about the strip being acquired than about how customers, tenants and trucks will get in and out afterwards. A driveway is closed or moved, a median goes in, the road becomes one with limited entry points. Whether any of that is paid for is one of the less uniform areas of condemnation law, and the answer often turns on exactly what the plans show.

## The general idea

In most states an owner whose land abuts a public road has a right of reasonable access to it. That right is generally treated as a property interest. It is not a right to any particular driveway, to access at every point along the frontage, or to a particular flow of traffic past the property.

Two broad categories follow from that. Where a project takes or substantially impairs access to the abutting road, many states allow compensation. Where a project only changes how traffic moves on the public road, most states treat it as regulation of the highway and allow nothing.

## What is usually not compensable

Courts in most states are reluctant to compensate for changes that every user of the road shares. Typical examples are:

- A median that prevents left turns in and out
- Conversion of a street to one-way traffic
- A longer or less direct route to reach the property
- Traffic diverted to a new road, so that fewer vehicles pass the site

An owner may lose real business from changes like these. That does not, by itself, make them compensable. The rules and their exceptions vary by state, so confirm them before telling a client either way.

## What may be compensable

The position is generally different where the property's own connection to the road is affected. Examples include closing the only driveway, leaving access that is not reasonably suited to the property's use, or converting the road to a controlled-access highway so that direct entry is lost. States differ on how severe the impairment must be, and on whether replacement access by a frontage road or a side street is enough.

## Why the partial taking matters

Many states treat an access change differently depending on whether land is also being acquired. Where there is a partial taking, the effect of the project on the remainder is part of the valuation, and impaired access may be considered as an element of damage to the remainder. Where no land is taken, the owner may have to bring a separate claim, and some states allow none. States also differ on whether damage must flow from the taking itself or may flow from the project as a whole. This is a point to settle early, because it decides which effects the appraiser may consider.

## Reading the plans

The offer letter rarely describes access. The plan sheets do. Check them for:

- **Driveways.** Which existing entrances remain, which are closed or moved, and how wide the replacements are.
- **Access control lines.** Whether the agency is acquiring access rights along any part of the frontage, which the plans and the deed usually show separately from the land.
- **Medians and turn lanes.** Where the openings are in relation to the entrances.
- **Grade.** Whether the new road sits higher or lower than the site, and how steep the rebuilt driveway will be.
- **Internal circulation.** Whether trucks can still turn, reach loading areas and leave once the frontage is narrower.

Where the plans are unclear, ask the agency in writing. Agencies will sometimes adjust a driveway location during design, and a request made early is more likely to be considered than one made after the plans are final.

## What to ask at intake

Ask the owner how the property is entered and left, by whom and in what vehicles. Find out whether any access permit, recorded easement or shared driveway agreement exists. If the property is leased, read the lease for what it says about access and parking. Photograph each entrance, and give the appraiser the plan sheets with the access changes marked.

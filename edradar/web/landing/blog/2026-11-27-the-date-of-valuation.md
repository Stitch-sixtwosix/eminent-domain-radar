An appraisal is an opinion of value on a particular day. In a condemnation matter that day is not chosen by the appraiser, the owner or the agency. It is fixed by law, and it can be months or years away from the day the agency's appraiser walked the property. Because markets move and properties change, the date deserves attention at the start of a matter and not only when the reports are exchanged.

## How the date is set

States differ, and this is a point to confirm before anything else. The date is commonly tied to an event in the acquisition or the lawsuit, such as:

- The filing of the condemnation action
- The date the agency deposits its estimate of compensation or takes possession
- The date title passes to the agency
- The date of the trial or valuation hearing

Some states use one event in every case. Others start with one and move to another in defined circumstances, for example where the case is not tried within a set period or where the agency takes possession early. The procedure the agency uses can also change the answer, so identify which procedure applies before assuming a date.

## The agency's appraisal has its own date

The appraisal behind the written offer is prepared before any case is filed, and its effective date is usually close to the inspection. If negotiations run long, that report may describe a market that no longer exists by the legal date of valuation. Values can fall as well as rise. When reading the agency's report, note its effective date first, and ask whether the agency will update it if the matter proceeds to litigation.

## What is fixed on that date

The date governs more than the choice of comparable sales. In general the appraiser values the property as it stood on that day, which includes:

- **Physical condition.** Buildings, improvements and site work as they then existed.
- **Legal condition.** Zoning, permits and approvals in place, and the reasonable prospects of change as the market would have seen them.
- **Occupancy.** Leases and rents in effect.
- **Market evidence.** Sales and listings near the date. Whether sales that closed after the date may be used, and for what purpose, varies by state.

## The project's own effect on value

Most jurisdictions hold that the property is valued without regard to changes in value caused by the project itself. If the announcement of a road depressed prices along the corridor, the owner is generally not to bear that loss, and if it raised them, the agency is generally not to pay for the increase. Applying the rule requires a view on when the project's influence began, and comparable sales inside the project area may need adjustment or may be unusable. The details differ by state and should be settled with the appraiser at the outset.

## Changes the owner makes while waiting

Owners often ask whether they should keep maintaining, leasing or improving a property once a project is known. There is no uniform answer. Some states limit compensation for improvements made after a certain point in the process, and a vacancy or deferred repair on the date of valuation may be reflected in the figure. Ask the owner to raise any planned construction, new lease or sale with counsel before committing to it, and keep a record of the property's condition with dated photographs.

## Delay between the date and payment

Where the owner is paid well after the property is valued or taken, many states provide for interest on some or all of the award. The starting point, the rate and the effect of an agency deposit all vary, so check the rule before estimating what a client will receive.

## What to do early

Identify the procedure and the likely date of valuation, and tell the appraiser in writing which date to use. Note the effective date of the agency's report. If the date may shift as the case goes on, plan for an updated appraisal.

*This article is general information, not legal advice. Eminent domain law differs by state.*

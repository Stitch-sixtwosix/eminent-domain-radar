---
title: Partial takings: why the land the owner keeps matters most
description: When an agency takes a strip rather than a whole parcel, the harder valuation question is what happens to the rest. A general outline of how that is analysed.
date: 2026-11-17
---
Most acquisitions for road, utility and drainage projects are partial. The agency needs a strip of frontage, a corner or an easement, and the owner keeps the rest. Owners tend to focus on the area being acquired, because that is the figure on the plan sheet. In many partial takings the larger question is what the project does to the property left behind.

## Two parts to the analysis

In general terms, compensation in a partial taking has two components: the value of what is acquired, and any loss in value to the remainder caused by the taking. The second is often called severance damages or damages to the remainder.

States differ in how they get there. Some use a before-and-after approach, comparing the value of the whole property before the taking with the value of the remainder afterwards. Others value the part taken and then add damages to the remainder as a separate step. The two methods can produce different results on the same facts, so the first thing to confirm in any matter is which method the jurisdiction uses and how its courts apply it.

## What can reduce the value of a remainder

The acquired area may be small while the effect on the remainder is not. Points an appraiser will commonly look at include:

- **Access.** A closed driveway, a new median or a change in grade can alter how customers, tenants or trucks reach the site.
- **Size and shape.** A remainder may be too shallow or too irregular for the use the whole parcel supported.
- **Site improvements.** Parking, signs, landscaping, wells, septic fields and drainage structures are often in the strip nearest the road.
- **Zoning conformity.** Losing frontage or area can leave a building closer to the right-of-way line than local setbacks allow, or leave a lot below a minimum size.
- **Proximity.** The improvement may sit much nearer to traffic than it did before.

Whether a given item is compensable is a matter of state law, and the answers are not uniform. Changes in traffic flow, loss of visibility and disruption during construction are treated very differently from one state to another.

## Benefits and cost to cure

Two further concepts affect the figure. Where the project is said to increase the value of the remainder, many states allow some form of offset, but they disagree on which benefits count and what they may be set against. And where damage to the remainder can be corrected, for example by rebuilding a driveway or relocating a sign, appraisers often measure the damage by the cost of the cure, where that is less than the loss in value it avoids.

## Easements and remnants

Not every acquisition is in fee. Permanent easements for utilities or slopes, and temporary easements for construction, are valued by what they take from the owner's use of the land and for how long. At the other extreme, a taking may leave a remnant with little practical use. Agencies will often offer to buy such a remnant outright, and an owner may want to raise the question if the agency has not.

## What this means at intake

For a partial taking, the early conversation with an owner should cover the whole property and how it is used, not only the strip on the plan. Useful questions are how people and vehicles enter the site, what sits in the area to be acquired, what the owner planned to do with the land, and whether the agency's appraisal addresses the remainder at all. The answers show whether the matter is a dispute about the price of a strip or about the future of the property.

*This article is general information, not legal advice. Eminent domain law differs by state.*
